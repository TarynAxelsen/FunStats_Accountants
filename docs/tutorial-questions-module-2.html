2.5 Question 1: Observational / Experiment

In recent years, the Therapeutic Goods Administration has introduced new guidelines for marketers of herbs and other natural substances who make health claims about their product. Brands of gingko extract claim to improve memory and concentration. A study of 230 healthy people over 60 years old were randomly assigned to a gingko pill or a placebo pill. All participants took tests for learning and memory before treatment started and again after six weeks.

(a) Is this an observational or experimental study? Explain your answer.

Ask yourself: did the researchers impose a treatment, or did they simply observe?
It is an experimental study because treatments (either a gingko or placebo pill) were imposed by the researchers on the subjects.

(b) What were the subjects and how many were there?

Who participated in the study, and how many of them were there?
The subjects were the 230 healthy people over 60 years old who participated in the experiment.

(c) Identify all factors and factor levels.

A factor is the variable being manipulated. The levels are the different values of that factor applied to subjects.
There is one factor: the pill.
The two levels of this factor are gingko and placebo.

(d) What was the response variable(s)?

What outcome was being measured? What did participants take tests on?
The response variables were the results on the learning and memory tests.

2.6 Question 2: Experimental Design

A researcher wants to know whether the difficulty of a task influences our estimate of how long we spend working at it. She designs two sets of mazes that subjects can work through on a computer — one set is easy and the other set is difficult. A group of 50 subjects are randomly allocated a set of mazes and work until told to stop (5 minutes, but subjects do not know this). They are then asked to estimate how long they worked.

(a) Draw a diagram to show the design of this experiment.

Start with the 50 subjects. Show how they are randomly split into two groups, what treatment each group receives, and what is then compared.
This is a completely randomised design:

50 subjects → Random assignment →
  Group 1 (25 subjects) → Easy mazes → Compare time estimates
  Group 2 (25 subjects) → Difficult mazes → Compare time estimates

(b) Have the four elements of good experimental design been used here?

Go through each of the four principles in turn: randomisation, control, replication, blocking. For each one, state whether it was used and why.
  • Randomisation: Yes — subjects were randomly allocated to either easy or difficult mazes.
  • Control: No — there is no group given no mazes (no treatment), so there is no control group.
  • Replication: Yes — there are 50 participants, so results for both treatments are replicated.
  • Blocking: No — subjects were not divided into groups with similar characteristics before treatments were allocated.

(c) *The researcher is interested to see whether gender has an effect on people's perception of how long it takes to do a difficult task. How could she account for this in her experimental design? Draw a diagram to show the revised design.

Which of the four experimental design principles would help control for the effect of gender? How would you apply it here?
Blocking by gender could be incorporated by dividing the 50 people into males and females first, then randomly allocating both easy and difficult mazes within each gender group:

Block 1 (females) → Random assignment → Easy mazes / Difficult mazes → Compare
Block 2 (males)  → Random assignment → Easy mazes / Difficult mazes → Compare

2.7 Question 3: Observational Study

A study found that senior citizens who report drinking diet soft drink regularly experience a greater increase in weight and waist circumference than those who do not drink diet soft drink regularly.

(a) From these results, can we conclude that drinking diet soft drink causes weight gain? Why or why not?

What type of study is this? What type of study is required to establish causation?
No, we cannot conclude causation. This is an observational study — no treatments were imposed by the researcher. We can only conclude that there is an association between drinking diet soft drink and weight gain. To establish causation, an experiment would be needed where the researcher randomly allocated participants to either drink or not drink diet soft drink.

(b) *What could be a potential lurking variable? Explain how it would impact both the explanatory and outcome variables.

A lurking variable affects both variables in the study but is not being measured. Think: what other factor might explain why someone both drinks diet soft drink AND gains weight?
The explanatory variable in this scenario is ‘type of drink’ (either diet soft drink or other). The outcome (or response) variables are ‘weight’ and ‘waist circumference’. There are multiple possible lurking variables. One possible lurking variable is health consciousness. Someone who is health conscious may avoid diet soft drinks and also exercise more (decreasing weight). Someone who is not health conscious may consume more junk food and exercise less (increasing weight). This creates an apparent association between diet soft drink and weight gain, even though the lurking variable is the real explanation.

85, 38, 92, 93, 100, 88, 78, 79, 96, 81, 62, 39, 77, 78, 83, 91, 90, 85, 99, 97

For this data and Figure 2.1 complete the following questions:

(a) Describe the distribution of Assignment Results in terms of shape, centre, spread and outliers.

Shape:

Look for the long tail — which direction does it point? Note the two very low scores (below 40) far from the main cluster.

Centre:

Look at where the tallest bars of the histogram fall — this is where most values are clustered.

Spread:

Report the range of the main cluster, excluding obvious outliers. Note: if you considered there are no outliers, 38–100 would also be acceptable.

Outliers:

Check the frequency (y-axis) of the lowest bar on the histogram. How many values are in that far-left bin?

(b) What centre and spread would you use to describe this distribution? Why?

Skewed distribution or outliers → use median (centre) and IQR (spread). Symmetric, no outliers → use mean and standard deviation.

(c) Calculate the mean of the data set:

Add all 20 values together, then divide by 20. The sum is 1631.

(d) Calculate the standard deviation of the data set:

Use the sample standard deviation (divide by n − 1 = 19). On most calculators this is the s or sx button.

(e) Calculate the median of the data set by hand:

Sort all 20 values. With an even number, the median = average of the 10th and 11th values in the sorted list.
Sorted: 38 39 62 77 78 78 79 81 83 85 85 88 90 91 92 93 96 97 99 100

(f) Why are the mean and median different/similar?

Which measure of centre is affected by extreme values (outliers)? Which is resistant to them?
The mean (81.55) and median (85) are not very different, but the difference is due to the skewness and the two outliers (38 and 39) which pull the mean downward. The median is resistant to outliers so it remains higher. In a left-skewed distribution, the mean will always be smaller than the median.

2.9 Question 5: Comparing Distributions

Figure 2.2: Side by side boxplots of housing price data (n = 545)

The figure above shows the distribution of house prices for 545 furnished and unfurnished properties in a large city. Compare the distributions of prices of furnished and unfurnished properties in fewer than 100 words. You should mention shape, centre, spread, and outliers in your discussion.

Work through shape, centre, spread and outliers systematically. Use the boxplot: the line inside the box is the median (centre); the box width is the IQR (spread); dots beyond the whiskers are outliers.
Shape: Both furnished and unfurnished house prices are right-skewed — there are some very high-priced properties pulling the distribution to the right.
Centre: The median price for furnished houses is higher than for unfurnished houses.
Spread: Furnished houses have a wider range of prices than unfurnished houses.
Outliers: Both groups have high-priced outliers, though furnished properties have more extreme values than unfurnished.

2.10 Question 6: Describing Distributions

Figure 2.3: Value of Australian exports ($ million)

Figure 2.3 shows the value of Australian exports (measured in $AUD million).

(a) What type of graph is this?

What type of graph is used when a quantitative variable is measured at regular intervals over time?

(b) In less than 100 words, interpret the information in this graph.

Describe what happened to Australian export values over time. Were there periods of stability? When did the rate of change accelerate?
The value of Australian exports was fairly stable between 1914 and approximately 1958, with only a slight rise during that period. After 1958, the rate of increase became exponential-growing faster and faster-with the exception of a short stable period around 1998–2002.
